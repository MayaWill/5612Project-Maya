Conclusion

Type 2 Diabetes, Cardiovascular disease, and Chronic Kidney Disease are all deeply intertwined conditions, each impacting the connections between the metabolic and cardiovascular system. Developing one condition raises the risk of developing another, as they frequently act as comorbidities. The aim of this project was to examine the role of Chronic Kidney Disease specifically in disease progression along the pathway of diabetes-to-cardiovascular disease and vice versa. Rather than simply acknowledging CKD as a risk factor shared by both conditions, the focus was on examining it as a specific part of the pathway that may drive diabetes into cardiovascular disease, and the reverse. By focusing on CKD as a mediator, the role of kidney function becomes more prominent in the metabolic processes underlying diabetes, as well as in the cardiovascular consequences that follow. Machine learning methods offer unique tools for disease prediction, specifically in classifying patients and estimating the probability of disease risk. For all three conditions, prevention is the most important aspect of keeping complications at bay, and understanding the interconnectedness of different biological indicators can provide clinically valuable information about how at risk a patient may be of disease progression. Each method applied provided a unique angle on the data, progressing toward both hard and soft classification boundaries, shared biomarker characteristics, and associative disease pathways. The relationship and shared risk factors between all three conditions are clinically well known, but tools that improve prediction could meaningfully support prevention, motivating to purpose of this project.

Individual conditions were examined across each method applied in this project. Chronic kidney disease consistently produced the most distinct biomarker signal of the three conditions, particularly through creatinine and albumin levels. These biological indicators reflect how well the kidneys are filtering the blood, as elevated creatinine indicates accumulating waste that the kidneys can no longer clear, while elevated urine albumin suggests progressive damage to the kidney's filtration pockets. These markers moved in highly predictable directions as kidney function declined, and this consistency meant that even relatively simple models could separate CKD patients from healthy ones with near-perfect accuracy. Diabetes was a similar case. HbA1c and fasting glucose are the two most clinically important biomarkers used to diagnose diabetes, and together they capture a complete picture of a patient's blood sugar profile. HbA1c reflects average blood sugar over the past two to three months, while fasting glucose captures the most recent eight-hour window. In models examining the diabetes dataset alone, these markers carried the majority of the predictive signal, allowing models to converge quickly and accurately. However, this also meant that performance on the diabetes dataset reflected the tight relationship between the features and the label construction rather than a more generalizable predictive finding. Cardiovascular risk had no single biomarker defining it, and the models that performed best were those capable of examining how multiple markers interact rather than crossing individual thresholds in isolation. The support vector machine required a flexible RBF kernel rather than a linear boundary, the decision tree used combinations of blood pressure and lipid markers at multiple levels, and the neural network required more training epochs to converge compared to the other datasets. This difference in boundary complexity makes clinical sense, as kidney dysfunction and blood sugar dysregulation are mechanistically direct processes with specific and measurable consequences, while cardiovascular disease accumulates downstream from many overlapping sources of metabolic and inflammatory stress, making it inherently harder to capture with any single model or feature set.

Across every method, the same biomarkers seemed to be relevant to more than a single condition. Kidney filtration markers showed up as cardiovascular risk indicators, such as HbA1c, which onnected to both kidney disfunction and heart disease. Along with this, lipid markers appeared across all three conditions simultaneously. Patients with multiple conditions consistently carried the highest risk in each analysis, which is expected given the known clinical pathways. At baseline for many datasets, the risk for developing cardiovascular disease was consistently high, as these same patients being examined for all three metrics were likely to have cardiovascaular risk. The direct diabetes to cardiovascular pathway explored in neural network approximates having cardiovascaular disease is over 16% more likely if the patien already has diabetes. Compounding this with a patient also having chronic kidney disease, renal signals continue into heart disease. Specifically, about 9.5% of the pathway from diabetes to heart disease overlaps with kidney damage as the middle step, proposing CKD as a partial mediator. Metabolic dysfunction from diabetes drives a patient to develop CKD nearly twice as much as cardiovascaular disease in the reverse direction. While the relationship is known to be bidirectional, this asymmetry indicates that the disease tends to move towards cardiovascuaalr disease more so, from metabolic dysfunction outward toward organ damage, specifically within the cardiovascaualr system. Controlled for diabetes, kidney dysfunction still elevates cardiovascular risk alone (but not as much as the compounding conditions). Once the kidneys are damaged, the cardiovascular risk exists alone, not requiring the blood sugar dysregulation to sustain it. The relationships between all conditions display a compounding risk factor, as each conditions raises the risk of another on its own, but further biological mechanisms are altered as they compound on one another.

But analysis of methods only goes so far if it fails to be applied in a clinical setting. Biomarker data is commonly collected through routine checkups, such as blood, saliva, and urine samples alongside physical exams. Given known risk factors like family history, comorbidities, lifestyle, and symptoms, biomarker analysis in a lab is a common and reliable way to detect not only present conditions but emerging ones that have not yet fully developed. If a patient is developing diabetes, the kidneys are likely among the first major organs to show wear, and that damage is unlikely to stay isolated. Early detection of kidney dysfunction in diabetic patients could therefore serve as a vital warning signal for cardiovascular risk that has not yet fully manifested. Biomarkers like creatinine, albumin-to-creatinine ratio, and HbA1c are already collected in routine blood and urine tests, meaning the information needed to identify this chain of risk is already available in standard clinical care. These diagnostic tests are generally accurate, but challenges arise with early detection, particularly when biomarkers are examined in isolation rather than together. A single elevated creatinine value means something different when accompanied by elevated HbA1c and low albumin than it does on its own, and current clinical workflows do not always capture those interactions systematically. Machine learning models trained on panels of biomarkers rather than individual values could help bridge this gap, providing risk estimates that account for the combined pattern of measurements rather than individual thresholds. This is where the findings from this project become practically relevant, not as a replacement for clinical judgment, but as a tool to detect early signals in the early stages of a disease progression pathway before any single marker has crossed a diagnostic threshold.

Throughout the project, a recurring limitation shaped the conclusions that could be drawn. Different datasets examined patients across varying axes of disease severity, and while the conditions studied are influenced by overarching biological indicators, each dataset prioritized different primary factors. Some datasets focused on physical characteristics such as age, sex, and weight, measurements that are clinically accessible without laboratory work and often used as first-pass indicators of patient health. Others, particularly the NHANES datasets used when examining disease progression rather than classification, focused on biomarkers derived from blood, urine, and saliva samples collected in a lab setting.NHANES datasets are well suited for examining health patterns across large, representative populations. A particularly useful aspect of these datasets as well is their shared participant identifier (SEQN) allows different surveys to be merged, linking kidney, diabetes, and cardiovascular measurements from the same individual. This was central to the mediation analysis, where matching patients across all three conditions was necessary to examine shared biomarker patterns within the same person rather than across separate groups. However, the most significant limitation of NHANES for this project is that it is cross-sectional, capturing a single snapshot of each patient's health at one point in time. This means it is impossible to determine whether a given biomarker value preceded the development of a condition or appeared as a consequence of it. Elevated creatinine in a diabetic patient, for example, could indicate that diabetes drove kidney damage over time, or it could reflect a pre-existing kidney condition entirely unrelated to their diabetes. Association can be observed but progression cannot. To draw meaningful conclusions about disease progression and mediation, longitudinal datasets would be needed, as these studies follow the same patients over time, collecting repeated biomarker measurements as continuous variables rather than single discrete metrics. This reflects the biological reality of how these conditions develop, changing over time due to numerous factors.

In summary, the consistency of findings across the different methods, all pointing towards the same shared biomarker patterns show a detectable biological signal in overlap in the pathway from diabetes to cardiovascular disease overlapping with chronic kidney disease. The strength of the diabetes-to-CKD pathway relative to the reverse, combined with the molecular overlap identified across clustering and PCA, makes this a well-motivated hypothesis for future investigation using longitudinal data and formal causal inference methods.
